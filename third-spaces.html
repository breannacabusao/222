Designing Third Spaces

from passion project to fate.

I kept noticing the same pattern. People graduating, landing new roles, moving to new cities, moving back home, and struggling to find community. Just like that the built-in structure of college disappeared. No more running into people on the way to class. No more spontaneous plans.

I couldn't stop thinking about it. What started as a semester-long passion project slowly turned into an attempt to solve it. Then, somewhere along the way, I found 222.

Origin story

I had the idea. So I did something about it.

After relentless thinking, I signed up for a design class with a specific thing I wanted to solve. I'd been watching people I went to school with and people I care about graduate and immediately get lost. Not dramatically lost, but a sort of lost that the word "loneliness" doesn't truly encapsulate.

No more walkable campuses where you bump into someone on the way to your next class and end up sitting at a café for 15 minutes debriefing your chaotic weekend in the city. Some were moving back home, unsure where to start. Some were moving somewhere new with a new job, trying to find their people from scratch. The connections that used to be accidental now required effort nobody knew how to put in.

This wasn't abstract research to me. I was living it alongside everyone else. I brought the idea to class, formed a team, and spent the semester trying to design a way through it. Interviewing people, sitting in spaces, watching how strangers did or didn't make contact, mapping what we found, building something real.

"Why build something from scratch when I can join a team already growing?"

Midway through the project I found 222. And I realized I didn't need to start something. I just needed to be part of the team that was already doing it right.

Written out problem statement
Soon to be graduates at universities often feel overwhelmed by post-grad life uncertainties. No resources for this transition into independence leads to students feeling anxious, stressed, and isolated. Action is needed to help seniors prepare for protecting their well-being.
The process

Six phases. Real rigor.

As PM, I owned the timeline, facilitated synthesis sessions, and made the calls when we disagreed on direction. I also did the work. Every phase below, I was in it.

01
User Interviews
Spoke with students and young professionals about where they felt most connected, and where they didn't. Two custom interview guides developed.
02
Ethnographic Research
Observed real behavior in cafés, libraries, plazas. Noted how people claimed space, avoided contact, or accidentally made it.
03
STEEP Analysis
Mapped Social, Technological, Economic, Environmental, and Political forces shaping how and where people connect today.
04
Affinity Mapping
Synthesized data into clusters. Identified recurring patterns around what made connection happen, or fail to happen.
05
User Personas
Built primary and secondary personas grounded in research. Kept every design decision tethered to real human needs.
06
Prototype + Present
Built a physical mock prototype. Presented to faculty and class. 10 minutes, scripted, drawn, defended.
Key findings

What the research told us.

Connection needs a reason
People don't talk to strangers in empty space. They need a shared activity, a question, a small excuse. The best third spaces engineer those excuses without making them feel engineered.
The threshold moment is everything
The moment you scan the room is when people decide if this space is for them. Most spaces fail here. No cue that signals belonging, no easy place to land.
Regularity over size
The spaces where relationships actually formed were small and consistent, not large and popular. Seeing the same person twice was more powerful than a thousand strangers.
Digital displaced, not replaced
Social media gave the illusion of connection. Our participants were more isolated than ever, and most couldn't name exactly why. The loneliness was quiet and ambient.
Final deliverable

10 minutes to make it real.

On the last day of class we had 10 minutes on the spot in front of faculty and classmates to draw out, script, and present our work. It covered our research journey, key insights, persona synthesis, and the physical prototype concept we developed. Below are the poster boards from the final presentation.

Looking back

What I actually learned.

Before this class I thought design was mostly about the prototype. It turns out design is 90% diverging. Sitting with the problem long enough to stop seeing the solution you already had in your head. That was hard for me. I've been thinking about this for a while. I had to actively let go.

Honestly? I walked in thinking I already knew the answer. I walked out knowing how to ask better questions.

What the research confirmed
People crave structure and direction. Most don't have it after graduation. The transition into adulthood isn't just logistically hard. It's isolating. And no one tells you that part.
What surprised me
When given a choice, people default to smaller groups. Not because they're shy. Because smaller feels safer and more real. That shaped everything about how we thought about the design.
What I'm taking forward
The adulting transition is inevitable. The uncertainty is inevitable. But we can engineer the conditions that make it easier. And that's exactly the problem 222 is already solving.
